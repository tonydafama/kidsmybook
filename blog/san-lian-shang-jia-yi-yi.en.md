I am a parent from the mainland. “Bookstore,” to me, used to mean shopping online. Then my daughter’s insect book was printed, and the studio said we could try physical shops — Joint Publishing, Commercial Press, Chung Hwa.

I will be honest: I did not expect much at first. You can sell online just the same. The day we actually walked into the Tsim Sha Tsui Joint Publishing, my daughter pulled my finger toward the shelf: “Mum — that’s my book.” She stood on her toes and touched the cover. An iPad never gives you that look.

I stood there thinking: “on the shelf” means, to a child, that your work is treated as real. Not a pile of scrap in a drawer at home. The same as other authors: sitting there for someone to walk past and pick up.

At Commercial Press I watched an older woman turn a few pages and tell her granddaughter, “This girl is about your age.” My daughter heard it and stood a little straighter. Being recognised by a stranger felt more solid than a hundred compliments from me.

For parents like me there is another layer. Hong Kong admissions cares about a child’s public presence — work that was not only practised behind a closed door, but put out where people can see it. A book actually selling at Joint Publishing tells a school the effort left a trail.

So if you are thinking about this, do not only think online. The copy you can hold is what a child remembers for life.
