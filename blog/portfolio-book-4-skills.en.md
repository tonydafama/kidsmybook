I am a GTP mother in Hong Kong. My husband works in AI at Science Park. I left a marketing-manager job in Shenzhen and followed. The first thing on the ground was finding a school place for our son.

A year later I had learned one thing: **everyone’s résumé looks the same.**

## Waking up in the waiting area

On the Primary 1 “knocking on the door” day I sat in the school waiting area. The parent ahead told the interviewer: gold in olympiad maths, Grade 8 violin, the swim team, community service. When it was our turn, what I had prepared was almost identical. By the third child the teacher was looking at the wall.

What my son likes is odd — he spends hours in the kitchen “inventing” recipes, including ingredients I thought were useless. I used to treat it as play.

Then I met an admissions consultant. She asked, “Does he have one thing he loves that you have always filed as completely useless?”

I said cooking. She said, “That may be the most valuable part.”

## How a book makes the story concrete

She suggested we turn his “kitchen experiments” into a formally published book with an ISBN. Not a print run to give away. Mentors and a university professor stay with the work: start from his observations and shape a book he actually helped write.

His book, *Science Experiments in the Kitchen*, uses flow charts he drew and notes he wrote. A professor helped straighten the structure. On interview day he held it and talked for fifteen minutes about why egg whites foam when you whisk them. The teacher asked, “He wrote this himself?” He nodded.

That moment beat ten certificates. A certificate says you are “good.” A book says you have thoughts of your own.

## Is this a luxury game?

No. The starter version took us a few months, at a pace a family can actually keep. The expensive part is the professor and the physical bookstore placement — and that is also why a school’s eyes light up.

If you are building a school portfolio too, ask: is there one thing that is theirs — not a class you signed them up for?
