In our first two years in Hong Kong I did what a lot of mainland parents do: I walked into a loop. Piano grades, olympiad medals, swimming certificates, Putonghua recitation. Every holiday meant another class. The drawer at home filled up.

For the first private-school interview I printed a colour portfolio. I thought the principal would see how capable my daughter was. They turned two pages, set it aside, and asked about ordinary life.

Later I talked with mothers who had volunteered at school. They named the secret: an interviewer sees more than ten children in a day. Each one arrives with almost the same list. After enough of that, the brain skips. More certificates do not mean distinctive. They mean the family was willing to enrol.

One interview I still remember. The boy ahead of us recited an English self-introduction. Fluent, eyes drifting. The principal asked, “What do you like doing most?” He thought, then said, “Piano.” Asked which pieces, and why he liked them, he could not answer. It was clearly practised for the interview, not loved.

I went home and asked myself: have I turned my daughter into a stack of paper? The moments she is actually happy are when she finishes drawing an insect or writes down one observation — not when she collects a certificate.

Certificates are not useless. Stacked too densely, they cover who the child already is. Interviewers want a person with their own thoughts, who can say why. No number of sheets replaces that.
